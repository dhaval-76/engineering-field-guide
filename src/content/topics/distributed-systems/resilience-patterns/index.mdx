## Why it matters

In a distributed system, something is always failing a little: a slow database, a GC pause, a deploy, a congested network. Whether that stays a small blip or becomes an outage depends on how callers react. Without protection, one slow dependency fills every caller's thread pool, the callers become slow, *their* callers fill up — a **cascading failure**. Retries without limits then multiply the load on whatever is already struggling. These patterns are how you keep the blast radius small.

<Callout type="analogy">
A house's electrical system. A fuse (circuit breaker) cuts off a faulty circuit before it burns the house down, and you test it again later. Separate circuits for the kitchen and the bedrooms (bulkheads) mean a broken kettle doesn't plunge the whole house into darkness. And you don't keep flipping a tripped switch every second (retry storm).
</Callout>

## Timeouts and deadlines

- **Every** network call needs a connect timeout and a response timeout — many clients default to infinite or very long values.
- Choose from the dependency's latency: a bit above its p99 under normal load, and well below your own SLA.
- **Propagate a deadline**: if the user's request has 2 s left, a downstream call shouldn't be allowed 5 s. gRPC does this natively; with HTTP, pass a remaining-time header or use a request-scoped budget.
- A timeout doesn't free the remote work — the downstream may still complete the operation. Combine with idempotency keys so retries are safe.

- Retry only **transient** failures (timeouts, 503, connection reset) and only **idempotent** operations — or ones made idempotent with a key.
- **Exponential backoff with jitter** (`random(0, base × 2^attempt)`, capped) spreads retries out instead of synchronising them into waves.
- **Cap attempts** (2–3) and use a **retry budget** (e.g. retries ≤ 10% of requests) so retries can't multiply load during an incident.
- Retry at **one** layer — usually closest to the failure — and honour `Retry-After`.

## Circuit breakers

<Diagram id="circuit-breaker-states" caption="A circuit breaker's three states. While OPEN, calls fail immediately without touching the dependency." />

The breaker watches recent calls (a count- or time-based sliding window). When the failure rate — or the rate of slow calls — crosses a threshold, it **opens**: calls fail immediately (or go to a fallback) for a cool-down period, freeing threads and giving the dependency room to recover. Then it **half-opens**, letting a few trial calls through; success closes it, failure re-opens it.

<CodeTabs id="resilience4j" title="Timeout, retry, circuit breaker and bulkhead around one dependency" />

Resilience4j applies its decorators in a fixed order — `Retry(CircuitBreaker(RateLimiter(TimeLimiter(Bulkhead(call)))))` — so the retry sees the breaker's fast failures and should not retry `CallNotPermittedException`.

## Bulkheads, rate limits and load shedding

- **Bulkhead**: a cap on concurrent calls to one dependency (a semaphore, or a dedicated thread pool). When it's full, new calls fail fast, and calls to *other* dependencies are unaffected.
- **Rate limiter** (client side): don't send more than a partner's quota allows.
- **Load shedding** (server side): when overloaded, reject early with 429/503 and `Retry-After` rather than accepting work you can't finish — queues that grow without bound only add latency before the inevitable timeout. Prioritise: shed analytics before checkout.
- **Backpressure**: bounded queues between stages ([Producer–consumer](../../concurrency/producer-consumer/)).

## Fallbacks and graceful degradation

| Fallback | Example | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| Cached / last known value | recommendations from yesterday | label staleness if it matters |
| Default value | "delivery in 3–5 days" | must be safe to be wrong |
| Hide the feature | page renders without reviews | the page must not depend on it |
| Queue for later | accept the order, charge asynchronously | needs a reliable queue and follow-up |
| Fail clearly | "payments are temporarily unavailable" | better than a hang |

A fallback must be simpler and more reliable than the call it replaces — a fallback that calls another remote service just moves the problem.

For writes that must not be lost — events to a broker that is down, calls to a partner API — the fallback is to keep the work durably and replay it later, in order and at a controlled rate: see [Surviving dependency outages](../failure-fallbacks/).

## Health checks

- **Liveness**: "is this process stuck?" — restart it if not. Keep it trivial; **don't** include dependencies, or a database blip restarts every instance.
- **Readiness**: "should this instance receive traffic now?" — false during startup, warm-up, shutdown, or when critical dependencies are unavailable.
- **Startup probes** for slow-starting JVMs, so liveness doesn't kill them mid-boot.

<Pitfalls>

- **No timeout** or default timeouts of minutes.
- **Retrying non-idempotent calls** — duplicate charges and orders.
- **Retries at every layer** and without jitter — retry storms that keep the outage going.
- **Circuit breaker without a timeout** — slow calls still hold threads until they finish.
- **Running time-limited calls on the shared `ForkJoinPool.commonPool()`** — one slow dependency starves unrelated work; give each bulkhead its own executor.
- **Fallbacks that call other fragile dependencies** or hide failures from monitoring.
- **Liveness probes that check the database** — a database incident becomes a restart storm.
- **Unbounded queues** in front of slow consumers.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Timeouts everywhere, tighter as you go deeper; propagate deadlines.
- Few retries, at one layer, with backoff + jitter, only for idempotent work.
- Circuit breakers fail fast; bulkheads contain; load shedding protects the core.
- Plan the degraded experience; keep liveness simple and readiness honest.

</KeyTakeaways>
