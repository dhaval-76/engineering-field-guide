## Why it matters

Configuration stores, schedulers, distributed databases and Kafka's controller all need one answer to "who is the leader?" and "what is the committed order of changes?" — even while machines crash and networks split. Consensus algorithms provide it. You'll rarely write one, but you'll run systems built on them, size their clusters, and reason about their failure modes — and leader election for your own services builds directly on them.

<Callout type="analogy">
A committee that only decides with more than half its members present. If the room splits into two groups, at most one group can have a majority, so the committee can never pass two conflicting decisions. The chair is elected the same way and only for a numbered session (a term); a chair from an old session has no authority once a newer session has started.
</Callout>

## Quorums

| Cluster size | Majority | Failures tolerated |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 0 |
| 3 | 2 | 1 |
| 4 | 3 | 1 (no better than 3) |
| 5 | 3 | 2 |
| 7 | 4 | 3 |

Use odd sizes. More nodes means more fault tolerance but slower writes (more acknowledgements) — 3 or 5 is typical, spread across availability zones.

## Raft

<Walkthrough id="raft-election" />

<Steps>

1. **Roles**: each node is a follower, candidate or leader. Time is divided into numbered **terms**; each term has at most one leader.
2. **Election**: a follower that hears no heartbeat within a randomised timeout becomes a candidate, increments the term and requests votes. Nodes vote once per term, only for candidates whose log is at least as up to date. A majority wins.
3. **Replication**: clients talk to the leader; it appends entries to its log and sends them to followers (`AppendEntries`, which doubles as the heartbeat).
4. **Commit**: an entry is committed once stored on a majority; committed entries are applied to the state machine in order on every node.
5. **Safety**: a node seeing a higher term steps down; the up-to-date-log voting rule guarantees a new leader already has every committed entry.

</Steps>

<CodeTabs id="raft-rules" title="The vote-granting rule and the majority commit rule from Raft" />

**Paxos** (and Multi-Paxos) solves the same problem and came first; **ZAB** powers ZooKeeper. Raft was designed to be easier to understand and implement, and is used by etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV and Kafka's KRaft.

Real-world points:

- Consensus needs timeouts: in a fully asynchronous network no algorithm can guarantee progress (the FLP result), so Raft stays **safe** always and makes **progress** when the network behaves well enough.
- Reads must go through the leader with a check that it's still leader (or a <Term id="lease">lease</Term>) to be linearizable — a deposed leader may not know yet.
- These algorithms assume crash failures, not malicious nodes; Byzantine fault tolerance is a different, costlier family (blockchains).

## Partitions and split brain

If a 5-node cluster splits 3 / 2, the side with 3 can elect a leader and commit; the side with 2 cannot — writes there fail. That's the CP choice in [CAP](../../databases/cap-and-consistency/). **Split brain** — two active leaders — happens in systems that elect without a quorum (two nodes that each assume the other is dead) or when an old leader keeps acting after losing its lease. Defences: quorum-based election, leases shorter than failure detection, and **fencing tokens** so resources reject the old leader ([Distributed locks](../distributed-locks/)).

## Leader election for your services

Run several instances of a scheduler but let only one act: campaign for leadership in etcd/ZooKeeper (or a Kubernetes `Lease` object, or a database lease row), keep the lease alive with heartbeats, and stop work the moment it's lost.

<Pitfalls>

- **Even-sized clusters** — 4 nodes tolerate no more failures than 3.
- **Stretching a cluster across distant regions** — every commit waits for cross-region round trips.
- **A leader that keeps working after losing its lease** — check the lease and use fencing.
- **Using a consensus store as a general database** — etcd/ZooKeeper are for small, critical coordination data.
- **Two-node "HA" pairs without a tie-breaker** — guaranteed split-brain on a partition.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Majority quorums make conflicting decisions impossible; 2f+1 nodes tolerate f failures.
- Raft: terms, randomised elections, log replication, majority commit.
- During a partition only the majority side makes progress.
- For app leader election, use a consensus-backed lease plus fencing — don't build your own.

</KeyTakeaways>
