## Why it matters

Navigation combines several different systems behind one screen: map tiles served to billions of devices, a road network of hundreds of millions of segments, shortest-path search fast enough to answer in milliseconds, live traffic derived from GPS pings, and arrival-time predictions. A plain Dijkstra over a continent is far too slow, so the interesting part is the precomputation and partitioning that make routing fast — and keeping it fresh as traffic changes.

<Callout type="analogy">
Planning a cross-country drive with a stack of maps. You don't study every village lane: you find the motorway near home, follow motorways between regions, and only look at local streets at the destination — and you listen to the radio for traffic jams on the way.
</Callout>

## Requirements

**Functional**: pan and zoom a world map; search places and addresses; route from A to B by car, walking or transit with ETA; turn-by-turn navigation with rerouting; live traffic; "restaurants near me".
**Non-functional**: hundreds of millions of daily users; map interactions in tens of milliseconds; routes in ~100–300 ms even across a country; ETA accuracy within a few percent; works on mobile networks.

## Estimates

- **Tiles**: zoom level z has 4ᶻ tiles; zoom 18 alone is ~69 billion, so tiles exist mainly where there's data and are generated on demand or in bulk for populated areas. Vector tiles (geometry the client renders) are small and styleable; raster tiles are simpler for clients. CDN hit rates are very high because everyone views the same popular areas.
- **Road graph**: a country has ~10⁸ nodes (intersections) and ~10⁸–10⁹ edges; compact adjacency arrays fit in memory on routing servers (tens of GB), partitioned by region.
- **Probes**: 10 M navigating devices sending a GPS point every few seconds ≈ millions of points/s → a streaming pipeline.

## Routing

<Walkthrough id="routing-search" />

<CodeTabs id="routing" title="Dijkstra and A* on a road graph" />

| Technique | Idea | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Dijkstra | expand nodes in order of distance from the source | correct, but explores a huge circle on long routes |
| **A\*** | prioritise by distance so far + an admissible estimate to the goal (straight-line distance ÷ max speed) | explores a narrow region towards the goal |
| Bidirectional search | search from both ends until they meet | roughly halves the explored area |
| **Contraction hierarchies** | precompute "shortcut" edges that skip unimportant nodes; queries only climb towards more important roads | continental routes in milliseconds (OSRM, GraphHopper) |
| Overlay / multi-level partitions (CRP) | precompute distances across partition boundaries; re-customise quickly when traffic changes weights | fast queries with frequently changing weights |

Edge weights are **travel times**, not distances, and depend on time of day; turn restrictions and costs are part of the graph. Routing servers are partitioned by region; long routes combine regions through the precomputed overlay.

## Traffic and ETA

<CodeTabs id="traffic-pipeline" title="From GPS probes to segment speeds" />

- **Map matching** snaps noisy GPS points to the road segments actually travelled.
- Per segment, compute current speed from recent probes (with privacy: anonymised, aggregated, minimum counts).
- **ETA** = sum over the route of segment travel times at the time you'll *reach* each segment: live speeds near you, historical/predicted speeds for later segments, plus learned corrections (models trained on actual trip durations).
- Updated weights are pushed to routing servers every minute or so; techniques like CRP re-customise precomputed data quickly.

## Places and nearby search

<CodeTabs id="geo-search" title="Nearby search with Redis GEOSEARCH and PostGIS" />

Spatial indexes map 2-D locations to cells or trees so that "within 2 km" reads only nearby candidates: geohash/S2/H3 cells (search the cell and its neighbours), quadtrees for uneven density, R-trees/GiST in PostGIS for shapes. Text + location ("pizza near me") is a search-engine query with a geo filter and distance-aware ranking.

## Navigation sessions

The client downloads the route geometry and upcoming tiles (and can cache regions offline), follows GPS locally, and contacts the server for rerouting when it detects a deviation or when traffic changes make a better route available. This keeps per-user server load low.

## When things fail

Interviewers almost always ask "what happens when X goes down?". Decide the degraded behaviour in advance:

| What fails | What users see | What the design does |
|---|---|---|
| Live traffic data stops | ETAs less accurate | fall back to historical speeds for that time of day; routing never depends on live data being present |
| A routing server for a region fails | a retry, then a route | several replicas per region hold the graph in memory; requests go to another replica |
| The phone loses connectivity mid-trip | navigation continues | the route and nearby tiles are downloaded at the start; re-routing resumes when back online |
| A tile CDN edge is down | slower map loading | the CDN routes to another edge or the origin; the app keeps cached tiles |

<Pitfalls>

- **Distance instead of travel time** as edge weights.
- **Plain Dijkstra over a country** on every request.
- **Two B-tree indexes on latitude and longitude** for radius queries.
- **Serving tiles from origin** instead of CDNs with long cache lifetimes.
- **Live traffic only** for ETA — the route's later segments will be traversed in the future.
- **Ignoring privacy** of location traces.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Separate tiles (CDN), routing (in-memory graph + A*/hierarchies) and places (geo indexes).
- Travel-time weights with live, historical and predicted components.
- Probe pipeline: map matching → segment speeds → weight updates.
- Spatial cells or trees for nearby queries; navigation mostly on the client.

</KeyTakeaways>
