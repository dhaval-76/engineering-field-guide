## Why it matters

Matching riders with drivers needs fresh locations for millions of moving drivers, updated every few seconds, and a match within seconds of a request — without offering one driver to two riders. The interesting parts are the write-heavy location pipeline kept in memory, a geo-index <Term id="sharding">sharded</Term> by city, dispatch that picks a driver atomically, and a trip state machine that survives disconnects and payments.

<Callout type="analogy">
A taxi dispatch office with a big wall map. Drivers radio in their positions every few seconds and the dispatcher moves their pins; a customer calls, the dispatcher looks at the nearby pins, offers the job to the best driver, and takes that pin off the board until the trip ends.
</Callout>

## Requirements

**Functional**: riders request rides and see fare estimates; nearby drivers receive offers; both see each other's live location; trip lifecycle and payments; ratings.
**Non-functional**: a match within seconds; location freshness within a few seconds; never assign one driver to two trips; highly available in each city; low latency for tracking.

## Estimates

| Quantity | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Active drivers | 1 M at peak, location every 4 s ⇒ **250,000 writes/s** |
| Payload | ~100 B per update ⇒ 25 MB/s ingest; live state 1 M × ~100 B ≈ 100 MB — tiny, fits in memory |
| Ride requests | 20 M trips/day ≈ 230/s average, ~1,000/s peak |
| Tracking fan-out | each active trip pushes the driver position to one rider every few seconds |

The data is small but changes constantly: optimise for update rate and freshness, not storage.

## The location index

Unlike [proximity search](../proximity-service/) over static businesses, drivers move every few seconds. Keep only the **latest** position per driver in memory, bucketed by cell, sharded by city (or by H3/S2 cell ranges) so each shard handles its own drivers. Drop drivers that stop reporting.

<CodeTabs id="driver-index" title="Driver location index: cell buckets, moves between cells, expiry, atomic claim and nearest-k ring search" />

- Updates are <Term id="idempotency">idempotent</Term> overwrites — losing one is harmless; the next arrives in 4 s.
- Replicate each shard (or rebuild from the stream) for failover; a few seconds of stale positions is acceptable.
- Publish the stream to Kafka for history, ETAs, fraud detection and surge.

## Dispatch

<Walkthrough id="dispatch" />

- **Atomic claims** prevent double assignment: the location service flips a driver from available to offered in one operation (or <Term id="redis">Redis</Term> `SET driver:{id}:trip {trip} NX`).
- **Offer timeouts** keep the flow moving; repeated declines widen the radius.
- **Batch matching**: in busy areas, matching all waiting riders and free drivers every couple of seconds as an assignment problem beats greedy first-come matching on total pickup time.

## Trips, tracking and payments

- **Trip service**: a state machine (REQUESTED → ACCEPTED → ARRIVED → IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETED / CANCELLED) persisted in a durable store with <Term id="optimistic-locking">optimistic concurrency</Term>; the same idea as the [LLD ride-sharing](../../low-level-design/ride-sharing/) model.
- **Tracking**: during a trip the driver's updates are also forwarded to the rider's <Term id="websocket">WebSocket</Term>; store the route for fare calculation and disputes.
- **Payments**: authorise an estimate at request, capture the final fare at completion; idempotency keys on every call ([Payment system](../payment-system/)).

## Pricing and surge

Aggregate open requests and available drivers per cell over a sliding window ([Real-time analytics](../realtime-analytics/)); when demand outstrips supply, raise a multiplier (smoothed and capped) to attract drivers and dampen demand. Show the price before confirmation and lock it into the trip.

## When things fail

Interviewers almost always ask "what happens when X goes down?". Decide the degraded behaviour in advance:

| What fails | What users see | What the design does |
|---|---|---|
| A location service shard dies | drivers briefly invisible in that area | drivers re-send locations every few seconds, so a replacement shard rebuilds its index within seconds |
| Two riders get offered the same driver | nothing | the driver is claimed atomically (compare-and-set on the driver's state); the loser re-dispatches |
| The driver's phone loses connectivity | the trip continues | the trip state is server-side; the app resyncs on reconnect; fares use server-recorded points |
| Payments are down at trip end | trip completes normally | the card was authorised at booking; capture is retried asynchronously |

<Pitfalls>

- **Writing every location update to a relational database.**
- **Straight-line distance for matching** — rivers, flyovers and one-way streets make ETA the real metric.
- **Non-atomic driver selection** — two riders get the same car.
- **No staleness check** — offers go to drivers who closed the app minutes ago.
- **A single global index** — shard by geography; cities are natural boundaries.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Live driver positions are a small, write-heavy, in-memory dataset sharded by geography.
- Dispatch = nearest candidates → ETA ranking → atomic claim → timed offer.
- Trips are durable state machines; tracking is a WebSocket stream; payments authorise then capture.
- Surge comes from streaming supply and demand per cell.

</KeyTakeaways>
