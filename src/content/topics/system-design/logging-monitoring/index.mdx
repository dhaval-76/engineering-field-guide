## Why it matters

With hundreds of services, "is it working, and if not, where?" can't be answered by reading log files on servers. An observability platform collects logs, metrics and traces from everything, keeps them affordably for the right amount of time, makes them searchable in seconds, and alerts people on what users actually experience. The difficulty is volume and cost — telemetry can rival production traffic — so the design is mostly about buffering, sampling, storage tiers and keeping label cardinality in check.

<Callout type="analogy">
A city's emergency monitoring centre. Sensors everywhere (metrics) show dials and trends; a dispatcher can follow one ambulance's route across districts (traces); incident reports (logs) give the details. The centre can't keep every second of every camera forever, so it keeps recent footage in full and summaries of the past.
</Callout>

## Requirements

**Functional**: collect logs, metrics and traces from ~500 services; search logs by time, service, level, trace id and text; dashboards for traffic, errors, latency and saturation; find which service made a request slow; alert the right team.
**Non-functional**: ~1 M log lines/s (bursting 10× during incidents), ~10 M active metric series, traces for ~100,000 requests/s; logs searchable within ~10 s; no loss during bursts; observability must keep working when production is failing; controlled cost.

## Estimates

1 M lines/s × ~500 bytes ≈ 500 MB/s ≈ 43 TB/day of logs before compression — the dominant cost. Seven days hot ≈ 300 TB (less with compression and sampling); older data in object storage. Metrics are far cheaper per insight: 10 M series × one sample per 15 s ≈ 670,000 samples/s, compressing to ~1–2 bytes per sample.

## The three signals, from the platform's side

What metrics, traces and logs are — and how a service should emit them — is covered in [Observability for application developers](../../distributed-systems/observability/). For the platform, what matters is that the three have very different **shapes and costs**, so each gets its own store:

| Signal | Shape of the data | Store built for it |
|---|---|---|
| Metrics | small numeric samples per series, queried by time range and label | Prometheus, Mimir/Thanos, VictoriaMetrics |
| Traces | spans per request, looked up by trace id, mostly sampled | Tempo, Jaeger, vendor APMs |
| Logs | large text events, searched by fields and text | Loki, OpenSearch/Elasticsearch, ClickHouse |

What the platform asks of every service: structured logs that carry the trace id, and metrics with **bounded** label values (no user ids in labels) — otherwise storage and query cost explode.

## Collection pipeline

- Services write JSON logs to stdout; node agents or OpenTelemetry Collectors ship them, add metadata (service, pod, region), drop noise, and batch.
- A **buffer** (Kafka) absorbs incident spikes; indexers consume at a sustainable rate and catch up afterwards. Applications never block on the logging pipeline — dropping debug logs under pressure beats slowing requests.
- Separate pipelines per signal, so a log flood can't delay metrics and alerts.

## Storage, retention and cost

- **Hot** (days): indexed for fast search; **warm** (weeks): compressed, slower; **cold** (months–years): object storage, queried rarely (compliance, audits).
- **Log levels and sampling**: keep all errors and warnings, sample high-volume INFO; never log per-request DEBUG in production by default.
- **Tail-based trace sampling**: decide after the trace completes — keep every trace with an error or high latency plus a small percentage of the rest.
- **Cardinality**: each unique label combination is a separate series; user ids, request ids or raw URLs as metric labels can create millions of series and break the metrics store. Put high-cardinality data in logs and traces instead.
- Redact secrets and personal data at the collector; access to logs is itself audited.

## Alerting

<Walkthrough id="incident-debug" />

- Define **SLIs** (e.g. share of checkout requests under 500 ms and non-5xx) and **SLOs** (99.9% over 30 days). The **error budget** is the allowed failure (0.1%).
- Alert on **burn rate** — how fast the budget is being consumed — with a fast window for severe incidents and a slow one for steady degradation. Fewer, more meaningful pages.
- Every alert links to a dashboard and runbook and routes to the owning team; non-urgent signals become tickets, not pages.

<Pitfalls>

- **Unstructured logs** — grepping free text across 500 services.
- **No trace context propagation** — logs from different services can't be joined for one request.
- **High-cardinality metric labels** (user id, full URL).
- **Writing logs straight into the search cluster** — the first incident spike drops data or backs up applications.
- **Alerting on causes** (CPU at 80%) instead of user-facing symptoms — alert fatigue.
- **Observability stack in the same failure domain** as the system it watches.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Metrics for detection, traces for location, logs for explanation — connected by trace ids.
- OpenTelemetry instrumentation; collectors + Kafka buffer; per-signal stores.
- Cost via levels, sampling (tail-based for traces), retention tiers and bounded cardinality.
- SLO burn-rate alerts with runbooks; keep observability independent of production failures.

</KeyTakeaways>
