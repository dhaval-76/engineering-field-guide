## Why it matters

Sending tomorrow's reminder emails, retrying failed payouts, running nightly reports: products are full of work that must happen at a certain time, exactly once in effect, even when machines crash mid-job. A distributed scheduler stores millions of due jobs durably, hands each one to exactly one worker at a time, recovers jobs from workers that died, retries with backoff, and never runs a job twice in a way that matters. It tests leases, <Term id="idempotency">idempotency</Term> and time-ordered storage.

<Callout type="analogy">
A hotel's wake-up call system. Requests are written in a book ordered by time; at each minute, staff take the due cards, mark them "in progress, back by 7:05" and make the calls. If a staff member disappears, the card's time runs out and someone else picks it up — so guests may occasionally get two calls, but never none.
</Callout>

## Requirements

**Functional**: schedule one-off jobs (run at T or after a delay) and recurring jobs (cron); priorities; retries with backoff; cancel and pause; dependencies (B after A succeeds); see job history.
**Non-functional**: 10 M executions/day; start within ~1 s of the due time; no job lost when a scheduler or worker dies; a job runs once in the normal case and never silently zero times; horizontal scale.

The single-process version (a <Term id="heap">priority queue</Term> and a worker pool) is the [LLD task scheduler](../../low-level-design/task-scheduler/); here the hard part is coordination across machines.

## Estimates

10 M/day ≈ 115/s on average, with sharp peaks on the hour (`0 * * * *` everywhere) — 10–50× bursts. Job rows ≈ 1 KB → 10 GB/day of history; keep active rows small and archive history.

## Claiming and leases

<CodeTabs id="job-claim" title="Claim due jobs with SKIP LOCKED, heartbeat, complete or retry" />

- A **<Term id="lease">lease</Term>** (owner, `lease_until`) marks a job as taken. A healthy worker **heartbeats** to extend it; if the worker dies, the lease expires and another worker can claim the job.
- Therefore a job can run **twice** (a slow worker whose lease expired is still running when another starts). Make job handlers idempotent, and for exclusive resources pass a **fencing token** (a version incremented on every claim) that the resource checks, so a stale holder's writes are rejected.
- Claim and execute in **separate** steps: commit the claim, run the job outside any database <Term id="transaction">transaction</Term>, then record the result.

## Retries, dead letters and recurrence

<CodeTabs id="job-schema" title="Jobs, schedules and executions" />

- On failure: `attempts + 1`, `run_at = now + backoff(attempts)` with <Term id="jitter">jitter</Term>, until `max_attempts`; then `DEAD` with the last error and an alert. Distinguish retryable errors (timeouts, 503) from permanent ones (validation).
- **Recurring jobs**: a schedule row holds the cron expression and time zone; each time an occurrence is created, the next occurrence is inserted (idempotently, with a unique `(schedule_id, scheduled_for)`), so missed occurrences after an outage can be detected and run or skipped by policy.
- **Overlapping runs** of a slow recurring job: choose per job — skip if the previous run is still active, or queue sequentially.
- **Dependencies**: store edges; when a job succeeds, decrement a pending-dependency counter on its dependents and release those reaching zero. Long multi-step pipelines belong in a [workflow engine](../workflow-engine/).

## As a work queue: visibility, priorities and fairness

The same design is a **distributed job queue** when jobs are due "now". Managed queues use the same ideas under other names, and interviewers often ask for them:

- **Visibility timeout = lease.** In SQS, a received message is hidden for the visibility timeout; the worker deletes it on success, or it reappears for another worker. Extending the timeout for a long job (`ChangeMessageVisibility`) is the heartbeat. A message received too many times moves to a dead-letter queue.
- **Priorities.** Strict priority (always take high first) starves low-priority work under load. Use separate lanes and poll them by weight (say 70 / 20 / 10), or reserve some workers per lane.
- **Fairness between tenants.** If one tenant enqueues a million jobs, a single FIFO queue makes everyone else wait behind them. With 10 workers and 100 ms jobs, a tenant who adds 10 jobs behind 10,000 others waits about 100 s; with a queue per tenant served round-robin, their jobs start within the next couple of rounds, about 0.2 s. Add a per-tenant concurrency cap so one tenant can't hold every worker.
- **Backpressure.** Bound queue depth per tenant and reject at enqueue (`429`) rather than accepting work you can't finish. Autoscale workers on the **age of the oldest job**, not the queue length: 10,000 one-millisecond jobs are no backlog, 100 one-hour jobs are.
- **Ordering per key.** When jobs for the same entity must run in order (all updates to one order), allow one in-flight job per key: SQS FIFO message groups, or a Kafka partition key.

## Scaling

- **Partition** the job table (or <Term id="redis">Redis</Term> sorted sets of due ids) by hash of job id; each scheduler instance owns some partitions (assigned via a coordinator or <Term id="consistent-hashing">consistent hashing</Term>) — no single leader scanning everything.
- **Time-bucketed** storage (jobs grouped by due minute) makes "what's due now?" cheap for far-future schedules.
- Decouple **scheduling** from **execution**: schedulers push due jobs to a queue (Kafka/SQS) and a separate worker pool runs them, so slow jobs don't delay triggering.
- Smooth thundering hours with jitter for jobs that don't need an exact second.

## When things fail

Interviewers almost always ask "what happens when X goes down?". Decide the degraded behaviour in advance. For services that publish events, the general patterns — circuit breakers, spooling, ordered replay — are in [Surviving dependency outages](../../distributed-systems/failure-fallbacks/).

| What fails | What users see | What the design does |
|---|---|---|
| A worker dies mid-job | the job runs a little late | its lease expires and the reaper makes the job due again; handlers must be idempotent because it may run twice |
| A scheduler instance dies | its jobs start late | partition ownership moves to another scheduler (leases or a coordinator) |
| The jobs database is down | no new jobs start | claims stop; running jobs finish; on recovery the backlog drains at a controlled rate so workers aren't flooded |
| A job keeps failing | nothing for other jobs | retries with exponential backoff and jitter, then a dead-letter state with an alert |
| A downstream the jobs call is down | jobs pile up | per-downstream concurrency limits and circuit breakers pause those jobs instead of burning retries |

<Pitfalls>

- **Delete-then-publish** of due jobs — a crash in between loses them; claim durably first.
- **Locks without expiry or heartbeats** — a dead worker holds a job forever.
- **Non-idempotent jobs** with <Term id="at-least-once">at-least-once</Term> execution.
- **One leader scanning all jobs** — a bottleneck and single point of failure.
- **Running jobs inside the claim transaction** — long transactions and held locks.
- **Ignoring time zones and daylight-saving changes** in cron schedules.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Durable jobs + atomic claims + leases with heartbeats.
- At-least-once execution: idempotent jobs and fencing tokens.
- Backoff retries, dead letters, explicit recurrence and overlap policies.
- Partition scheduling; separate triggering from execution.

</KeyTakeaways>
