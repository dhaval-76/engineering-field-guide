## Why it matters

A payment system can be carefully idempotent and still be wrong. A webhook never arrives, a provider charges a fee nobody configured, a refund is settled twice, a currency conversion rounds differently, or a bug writes the wrong amount. None of these raise an error at the time. **Reconciliation** is the independent check that finds them: it compares what we think happened with what the provider and the bank say happened, line by line, every day.

Finance teams need it to close the books, regulators and auditors expect it, and engineers rely on it as the last safety net. It also makes an interesting design problem: large batch data, unreliable external files, fuzzy matching, and a workflow for humans to fix what the machine can't.

<Callout type="analogy">
Checking your bank statement against your receipts at the end of the month. Most lines match by date and shop name. A few don't: a charge you don't recognise, a refund that never came, a subscription billed twice, a café that charged a tip you forgot. Some receipts aren't on the statement yet because they're still pending. You don't edit the statement; you note each difference and chase it until it's explained.
</Callout>

## Requirements

**Functional**:
- Import settlement reports from several payment providers (files over SFTP, or APIs) and bank statements (MT940 or ISO 20022 camt.053 files).
- Match them against our payments, refunds and payouts.
- Report breaks by type, amount and age; let operations staff resolve them, with rules that resolve common ones automatically.
- Produce daily summaries for finance: matched totals, fees, open breaks.

**Non-functional**:
- 5 million payments a day across providers.
- A day's run finishes within an hour of the last file arriving.
- Re-running a day, or re-importing a file, never creates duplicate results.
- Every resolution is auditable; nothing is deleted or edited in place.

## Estimates

| Quantity | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Rows per day | 5 M payments + 5 M settlement lines + a few thousand bank lines (payouts are batched) |
| Raw data | ~300 bytes per line ⇒ ~3 GB/day of files, kept for years for audit |
| Matching | 1 M × 1 M in-memory hash match: **140–240 ms** in Java (measured, JDK 22) |
| Breaks | typically well under 1% of lines ⇒ thousands a day, which is what people actually work on |

The matching itself is cheap; a single machine or a single SQL query handles a day. The difficulty is data quality, timing differences and the resolution workflow.

## High-level design

<Diagram id="recon-pipeline" caption="Files are kept raw, parsed and deduplicated, normalised to one format, matched in passes, and anything left becomes a typed break for rules or people to resolve with adjusting ledger entries." />

- **Ingest**: a scheduled job (or a webhook from the provider) fetches each file, stores it unchanged in object storage with its checksum, and records it in a `files` table. A file seen before is skipped. Each line is inserted with its natural key `(source, line id)`, so a partial import can simply be run again.
- **Normalise**: convert to minor units and ISO currency codes, parse dates into a value date, and split fees, FX and adjustments into separate lines. Every provider's format is different; adapters per source keep that mess out of the matcher.
- **Match**: run per day and per source, after the expected files have arrived (or at a cut-off, reporting missing files as their own break).
- **Breaks and resolution**: a queue of breaks with owners, notes and an audit trail.

## Matching in passes

<Walkthrough id="recon-run" />

<CodeTabs id="recon-matcher" title="A two-pass matcher: exact reference, then amount within a date window; the rest is in transit or a break" />

1. **Exact reference.** We send our payment id to the provider as a reference or metadata field, and it comes back in the settlement report. This should match the vast majority of lines. A reference that matches with a different amount or currency is an `AMOUNT_MISMATCH`; the same reference twice is a `DUPLICATE`.
2. **Amount, currency and a date window.** For lines without a usable reference (some bank transfers, older providers), pair by amount and currency within a couple of days, closest date first.
3. **Many to one.** Providers pay out in batches: one bank credit equals the sum of many payments minus fees, refunds and chargebacks. Match the payout report's lines to payments, then the payout total to the bank line.

How safe is pass 2? Measured on one million payments where 2% of settlement lines had no reference:

| Distinct amounts | Wrongly paired, among amount matches |
|---|---|
| ~1,000,000 (amounts nearly unique) | 0.4% |
| 1,000 price points | 92.8% |
| 50 price points | 99.7% |

The totals still balance when amounts are swapped, but refunds, disputes and customer support then point at the wrong payment. So treat amount matches as **suggestions** unless the amount is unique within its window, and use more fields when the source has them (card last four digits, authorisation code, customer id).

## Timing: in transit isn't a break

Payments settle a day or more after they're captured, cut-off times differ between provider and bank, and weekends and holidays add more. An unmatched payment younger than the expected settlement window (say 3 days for cards) is **in transit**; it becomes a break only when the window passes. The same applies the other way: a settlement line for a payment captured just before midnight may arrive before our ledger's day is closed.

## Doing it in SQL

<CodeTabs id="recon-tables" title="Idempotent tables and a full outer join that classifies every row" />

A `FULL OUTER JOIN` on the reference returns matched pairs plus the rows missing on either side in one pass, so the whole of pass 1 is a single query that a database runs as a hash join. Two details matter for re-runs:
- Raw lines have a primary key from the source, so re-importing a file inserts nothing new.
- Breaks have a unique index so `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` makes re-running a day harmless. Testing this on PostgreSQL showed a trap: with a plain `UNIQUE (…, payment_id, line_id)`, one-sided breaks were inserted again on every run, because **NULLs never conflict** in a unique constraint. The index on `coalesce(…)` (or `UNIQUE NULLS NOT DISTINCT` on PostgreSQL 15+) fixes it.

## Breaks and resolution

| Break | Usual cause | Typical resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Amount mismatch | an unexpected fee, FX rounding, a partial capture | auto-rule for known fee schedules; otherwise post a fee entry and raise it with the provider |
| Duplicate settlement | double capture, a retried request without an idempotency key | refund or record a payable; fix the idempotency bug |
| Missing in ledger | a lost webhook, a payment made outside our system | look up the provider's record; create the missing payment through the normal flow, or park it in a suspense account |
| Missing at provider | a failed capture we recorded as successful, a cancelled payment | query the provider's API; reverse our entry if it never happened |
| Missing file | the provider didn't deliver | alert and retry fetching; never treat its absence as "nothing settled" |

- **Never edit or delete ledger entries.** Every fix is a new entry (a fee, a reversal, a transfer out of suspense) with a link to the break, so the books always explain themselves.
- **Rank breaks** by amount and age, and alert when a type suddenly spikes: a jump in `MISSING_IN_LEDGER` usually means webhooks are broken right now, which is worth fixing before the end of the day.
- **Auto-resolve** the boring cases with explicit, audited rules ("PSP-A card fee of 2% ± 1 paisa"), and leave the judgment calls to people.

## Scaling

- Partition the work by **source and day**: each run is independent, so different providers' runs go in parallel and a late file only delays its own source.
- One provider with hundreds of millions of lines a day still fits a hash join; beyond one machine, partition by hash of the reference so both sides of a match land on the same worker (a Spark or SQL engine does this for you).
- Keep raw files and match results for the audit period (often seven or more years), in cheap storage partitioned by date.

## When things fail

Interviewers almost always ask "what happens when X goes down?". The general fallback patterns are in [Surviving dependency outages](../../distributed-systems/failure-fallbacks/).

| What fails | What users see | What the design does |
|---|---|---|
| A provider's file is late or missing | finance sees the day as incomplete | the run waits until a cut-off, then reports a missing-file break; other sources reconcile normally |
| A file is delivered twice or corrected | nothing | the checksum and line keys skip duplicates; a corrected file supersedes lines by key, and the day is re-run |
| The run crashes halfway | a delayed report | runs are idempotent: start the day again from the raw files |
| A parser change misreads a field | a sudden spike of breaks | alerts on break rates by type; fix the adapter and re-run from the stored raw files |
| The ledger database is under load | nothing | matching reads from a replica or a daily export, never the primary on the payment path |

<Pitfalls>

- **Matching on amount alone** when amounts repeat — silently pairs the wrong payments.
- **Treating unsettled payments as breaks** — floods the queue every day; apply a settlement window.
- **Editing or deleting ledger rows** to make numbers match — destroys the audit trail.
- **Not keeping raw files** — a parser bug can't be fixed by re-running.
- **Floats for money** or mixing currencies — use integer minor units with a currency code.
- **Unique constraints on nullable columns** for idempotency — NULLs don't conflict.
- **Reconciling only totals** — equal sums can hide offsetting errors.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Reconciliation independently checks ledger, provider and bank against each other, every day.
- Keep raw inputs, dedupe by natural keys, and make every run repeatable.
- Match by exact reference first; amount-and-date matching is a suggestion unless unique; batch payouts match many to one.
- Wait out the settlement window, then turn leftovers into typed, aged, sized breaks.
- Fix with new adjusting entries, never by editing history.

</KeyTakeaways>
