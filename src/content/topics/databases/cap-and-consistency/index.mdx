## Why it matters

As soon as data lives on more than one machine, "what does a read return?" stops having an obvious answer. CAP and consistency models give you the vocabulary to answer it precisely — and to decide, per feature, what the product can tolerate: a like count that's a few seconds behind is fine; a seat sold twice isn't. Interviewers listen for whether you apply these trade-offs to specific operations rather than reciting "pick two of three".

<Callout type="analogy">
Two bank branches share one ledger over a phone line, and the line goes dead. A customer asks the north branch for a withdrawal. The branch can refuse until the line is back (consistent, not available) or pay out and reconcile later, risking an overdraft (available, not consistent). While the line works, every withdrawal costs a phone call to the other branch — that waiting is PACELC's latency.
</Callout>

## CAP, stated precisely

<Walkthrough id="partition-choice" />

- **Partition tolerance isn't optional.** Networks do split, and nodes do pause long enough to look split. So the real choice is what happens *during* a partition.
- **C** is **linearizability**: the system behaves as if there were one copy, and once a write completes, every later read returns it (or something newer).
- **A** means *every* request to a non-failing node gets a non-error response — a strict definition; many "CP" systems are highly available in practice and only refuse requests on the minority side of a partition.
- CAP says nothing about latency, about what happens when there's no partition, or about weaker-but-useful guarantees. That's why it's a starting point, not a design tool.

## PACELC: the everyday trade-off

> If there is a **P**artition, choose **A**vailability or **C**onsistency; **E**lse, choose **L**atency or **C**onsistency.

Partitions are rare; latency is constant. A strongly consistent write must reach a quorum, perhaps in another zone or region, on every request. Systems that favour latency answer from the nearest replica and replicate asynchronously.

| System (default settings) | Partition | Else | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| PostgreSQL/MySQL, single primary + async replicas | C (writes go to the one primary) | L for replica reads, C for primary reads | the application picks per query |
| Cassandra / ScyllaDB | A | L | `QUORUM` reads and writes move it towards C |
| DynamoDB | A | L (eventually consistent reads) | strongly consistent reads and transactions are options |
| MongoDB (majority write concern) | C | C | `readPreference: secondary` trades towards L |
| etcd, ZooKeeper, Consul | C | C | consensus systems; used for coordination and leader election |
| Spanner, CockroachDB | C | C | consensus per range; Spanner uses TrueTime clocks for external consistency |

<Callout type="warning">
Avoid calling a database "CP" or "AP" outright. Most are configurable per keyspace, table or request — Cassandra with `ALL` refuses requests during a partition; DynamoDB with strongly consistent reads does too. Classify the **operation and its settings**.
</Callout>

## Consistency models

<Diagram id="consistency-ladder" caption="Stronger models are easier to program against and cost more coordination, latency and availability." />

| Model | Guarantee | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Linearizable** (strong) | one global order matching real time; reads see the latest completed write | balances, inventory reservation, leader election, unique usernames, locks |
| **Sequential** | all nodes see operations in the same order, not necessarily real-time order | replicated logs |
| **Causal** | if B depends on A (a reply to a comment), everyone sees A before B | social feeds, chat, collaborative apps |
| **Read-your-writes** | you see your own writes | profile edits, "my orders" |
| **Monotonic reads** | you never see older data after newer | any paginated or refreshed view |
| **Bounded staleness** | at most *k* versions or *t* seconds behind | dashboards, catalogues |
| **Eventual** | replicas converge once writes stop | counters, likes, recommendations, analytics |

Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads, causal for one user) are often enough for user-facing features and are cheap to provide with sticky routing — see [Replication](../replication/).

## How strong consistency is achieved

- **Single leader** for each piece of data: every read and write for that item goes through one node (one primary, or one leader per shard). Simple, but the leader must be chosen safely.
- **Quorums**: with N replicas, writes to W and reads from R with W + R > N overlap ([Replication](../replication/)). Quorums alone don't give linearizability in every failure case; consensus does.
- **Consensus** (Raft, Paxos, Zab): a majority of nodes agrees on each entry of a replicated log and on who the leader is. A cluster of 3 tolerates 1 failure, 5 tolerates 2; the minority side of a partition can't make progress, which is exactly the CAP "C" choice.

## Applying it in a design

1. **List the operations**, not the database: "reserve seat", "show seat map", "post comment", "count likes".
2. **Ask what goes wrong if this read is stale** or this write is lost: money, double booking, legal exposure — or a slightly old number.
3. **Pick the weakest model that's safe** for each: strong for the reservation, read-your-writes for the user's own bookings, eventual for the seat-map overview with a final check at purchase.
4. **Say what happens during a partition**: the reservation fails with a retryable error; the browsing keeps working with cached data.

<Pitfalls>

- **"Pick two of three"** — partitions aren't optional; the choice only applies during one.
- **Confusing CAP's C with ACID's C** (constraint validity) or with isolation levels.
- **Labelling whole products CP/AP** regardless of configuration.
- **Strong consistency everywhere** — paying cross-zone latency on reads that could be stale.
- **Eventual consistency for invariants** — double-spends and overbooking.
- **Even-sized consensus clusters** — 4 nodes tolerate no more failures than 3.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- CAP: during a partition, refuse (C) or answer possibly stale (A). PACELC adds the always-present latency trade-off.
- Consistency is a ladder; choose per operation, as weak as is safe.
- Session guarantees cover most user-facing needs cheaply.
- Strong consistency = a single leader or consensus with majority quorums.

</KeyTakeaways>
