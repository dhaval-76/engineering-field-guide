## Why it matters

Senior engineers aren't distinguished by knowing more technologies but by choosing between them well and explaining why. Interviewers deliberately ask open-ended questions with no single right answer, then push back on your choice. What they grade is the reasoning: which factors you considered, whether the decision follows from the requirements, and whether you know what you gave up.

<Callout type="analogy">
Choosing a car: a sports car, an estate and a city hatchback are all "good". The right one depends on whether you're carrying a family, commuting in a city or racing on weekends — and every choice gives up something. A good salesperson asks how you'll use it before recommending one.
</Callout>

## A four-step framework

<Steps>

1. **Name the options** — at least two real ones: "Kafka or a table-backed job queue."
2. **Pick the criteria that matter for *this* problem** — throughput, ordering, consistency, latency, operability, cost, team familiarity. Tie them to requirements and numbers.
3. **Compare on each criterion** — briefly, honestly; each option should win something.
4. **Decide and state the cost** — "I'll use Postgres with SKIP LOCKED: 200 jobs/s doesn't justify running Kafka; if we reach tens of thousands per second or need replay, I'd move to Kafka."

</Steps>

## The trade-offs that keep coming back

| Axis | One side | Other side | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistency vs availability / latency | correct immediately, may refuse or wait | always answers, may be stale | what breaks if this read is stale? |
| Sync vs async | simple, immediate result, coupled | decoupled, resilient, eventually consistent | does the caller need the result now? |
| Push vs pull | low latency, sender controls rate | receiver controls rate, simpler failure handling | who should absorb bursts? |
| Normalised vs denormalised | one source of truth, writes simple | fast reads, copies to keep in sync | read- or write-heavy? |
| Precompute vs compute on read | fast reads, wasted work, staleness | fresh, slower reads | read:write ratio? |
| Monolith vs services | simple, transactional | independent deploy/scale, distributed problems | how many teams? |
| Build vs buy / managed | control, fit | speed, less operations, cost, lock-in | is this our core business? |
| Optimistic vs pessimistic locking | no waiting, retries on conflict | waiting, no retries | how often do conflicts happen? |
| Strong typing / schemas vs flexibility | safety, evolution rules | fast change | how many consumers? |
| Cost vs performance | cheaper, slower | faster, more expensive | what is the latency worth? |

## Phrases that carry reasoning

These aren't magic words — they work because each one states a reason or a limit:

- "Given the requirement that…, I'd choose…, because…"
- "The cost of this choice is… — acceptable here because…"
- "This works up to roughly … ; beyond that I'd …"
- "Two options: A is simpler, B handles X better. Since X isn't a requirement yet, A."
- "I'm assuming …; if that's wrong, the design changes like this: …"
- "Let me check that against the numbers: … per second × … bytes ≈ … — fine for one node."

## When the interviewer pushes back

- **Listen for the new constraint.** "What if traffic is 100× higher?" or "Isn't that over-engineered?" usually signals a requirement you should adapt to, not a trap to resist.
- **Defend with reasons, not repetition**: restate the requirement the choice serves and the alternative's cost.
- **Change your mind visibly** when the argument is good: "You're right — with that constraint, B is better because…". Interviewers value updating on evidence.
- **Simplify on request**: separate essential components from those added for anticipated needs, and drop the latter.

<Pitfalls>

- **"It depends" with nothing after it.**
- **Listing pros and cons without deciding.**
- **One-sided comparisons** where your choice wins everything.
- **Choosing by fashion** ("microservices because they scale") rather than requirements.
- **Ignoring operations and cost** — who runs it, what it costs per month.
- **Digging in when given a new constraint** instead of adapting.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Options → criteria → comparison → decision with its cost.
- Tie every choice to a requirement or a number, and say when you'd revisit it.
- Each option should win something; say what you're giving up.
- Treat pushback as new information: defend with reasons or adapt openly.

</KeyTakeaways>
