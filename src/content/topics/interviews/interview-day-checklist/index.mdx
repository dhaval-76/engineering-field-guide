## Why it matters

Most interviews aren't lost on knowledge but on execution: starting to code before understanding the problem, running out of time, going silent, or forgetting edge cases that you'd catch at work. A plan for each round and a few habits make your normal skill visible under pressure.

<Callout type="analogy">
A pilot's pre-flight checklist. Pilots know how to fly; the checklist exists because stress makes skilled people skip obvious steps. A round plan does the same job: you don't have to decide under pressure when to stop clarifying or when to start testing, because you decided it beforehand.
</Callout>

## Before the interview

<Steps>

1. **Setup (remote)**: camera, microphone and screen sharing tested; the coding environment or whiteboard tool opened beforehand; notifications off; a charger and water nearby.
2. **Notes**: one page — your introduction, your project's diagram and numbers, the plan for this round type, and questions to ask them.
3. **Warm up** with one easy problem or by saying your introduction aloud. Don't learn new topics on the day.
4. **Know the logistics**: interviewer names, round type, duration, and whether you'll need to run code.

</Steps>

## Habits during every round

- **Clarify first**, and write down what you agreed: inputs, constraints, scale, edge cases, what's out of scope.
- **State the plan before executing** — the interviewer can redirect you before you spend twenty minutes.
- **Think aloud**, but in conclusions, not noise: "A heap gives the k smallest in O(n log k)…".
- **Checkpoint your time**: half-way through, you should be implementing (coding) or past the high-level design (system design).
- **Verify unprompted**: trace an example, then edge cases (empty, one element, duplicates, overflow, concurrency).
- **Close loops**: if you defer something ("validation later"), come back to it or say explicitly what you'd do.
- **Use hints gracefully**: thank, connect it to your approach, adjust.

## When you get stuck

<Steps>

1. **Restate** the problem and constraints in your own words.
2. **Shrink it**: work through a tiny example by hand and look for the pattern.
3. **Brute force first**: say it, give its complexity, then improve the bottleneck.
4. **Change representation**: sort it, use a map, think of it as a graph, go from the end.
5. **Ask a focused question**: "Is it fair to assume the input fits in memory?" rather than "Can you give me a hint?"

</Steps>

## After the round

Write down the questions and how you answered while it's fresh; note what went well and one thing to improve. Then let it go — the next round is independent, and interviewers can't see how you think the last one went.

<Pitfalls>

- **Coding in the first two minutes.**
- **Long silences** while coding.
- **Ignoring concurrency** in a design that obviously has shared state.
- **No testing** — waiting for the interviewer to find the bug.
- **Over-engineering** a design before the core works.
- **Arguing with the interviewer** instead of exploring their concern.
- **Letting a bad round affect the next one.**

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Prepare the setup and a one-page plan; don't cram.
- Clarify, plan aloud, build, verify, discuss trade-offs — with time checkpoints.
- When stuck: restate, shrink, brute force, change representation, ask precisely.
- Review each round briefly, then reset for the next.

</KeyTakeaways>
