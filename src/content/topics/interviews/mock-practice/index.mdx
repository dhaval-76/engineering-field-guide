## Why it matters

Knowing how to solve a problem at your desk and solving it in 45 minutes while a stranger watches are different skills. Reading solutions builds recognition, not recall; solving without speaking leaves you unpractised at the part interviewers grade most. Mock interviews close that gap — but only if they recreate the pressure and you review them honestly.

<Callout type="analogy">
Musicians don't prepare for a concert only by listening to recordings or practising in fragments. They run the whole piece, at tempo, in front of someone — and listen back to the recording.
</Callout>

## Making practice transfer

<Steps>

1. **Unseen problem.** Pick from a list you haven't looked at, or have a partner choose. Familiar problems test memory.
2. **Real time limit.** 40–45 minutes for coding, LLD and HLD; 60–90 for machine coding.
3. **Out loud.** Explain as you go — to a partner, or to a recording.
4. **Realistic tools.** Plain editor or whiteboard tool for coding rounds; a diagramming tool for design; no searching.
5. **Review immediately.** Score with the rubric, note the one thing that would have changed the outcome.
6. **Re-attempt later.** A few days on, solve the failed problem again from scratch.

</Steps>

## Scoring rubric

<CodeTabs id="mock-rubric" title="A rubric for coding, design and behavioural mocks" />

Score 1–4 per row. The goal isn't a high total — it's finding the lowest row and practising that specifically: if clarification is weak, start each problem by writing three questions before anything else; if verification is weak, always finish by tracing two test cases aloud.

## Peer mocks

- **Swap roles** — interviewing someone else teaches you what interviewers see: long silences, unexplained jumps, untested code.
- The interviewer should **interrupt** like a real one: add constraints, ask "why", push back on a choice.
- Feedback in the rubric's terms, with **one** top improvement.
- Record (with consent) and watch the first and last five minutes — openings and wrap-ups are where most points are won or lost.

## Reviewing a recording

Watch for: time before the first clarifying question; silences longer than ~30 seconds; restating the plan before coding; whether you tested your code unprompted; filler phrases and vague words; how you reacted to a hint.

<Pitfalls>

- **Solving familiar problems** and mistaking recall for skill.
- **Practising silently** — then freezing when asked to explain.
- **No time limit**, or quietly extending it.
- **Re-reading solutions** instead of re-solving.
- **Only practising your favourite round type.**
- **Skipping the review** — a mock without feedback is just another attempt.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Real conditions: unseen, timed, aloud, reviewed.
- One rubric, every time; fix the lowest score next.
- Re-solve failures cold; read solutions only after a genuine attempt.
- Practise every round type, and interview others too.

</KeyTakeaways>
