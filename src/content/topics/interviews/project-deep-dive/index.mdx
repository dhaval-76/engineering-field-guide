## Why it matters

The first minutes set the direction of an interview: interviewers follow up on whatever you mention. A focused introduction steers them to your strengths; a list of fifteen technologies invites fifteen probing questions. The project deep dive then tests things no puzzle can — whether you understood the system you worked on, made decisions for reasons, and can show impact.

<Callout type="analogy">
Your introduction is a restaurant menu: everything on it can be ordered. Don't list dishes your kitchen can't cook well.
</Callout>

## The 90-second introduction

<Steps>

1. **Who you are now** — role, years, domain: "Backend engineer, four years, payments at a fintech."
2. **One project** — what it does and its scale: "I own the payout service: 400,000 payouts a day to 30 banks."
3. **One decision you're proud of**, with its effect: "I replaced the nightly batch with an event-driven pipeline and idempotent bank calls — payout time dropped from 24 hours to 4 minutes, and duplicate payouts went to zero."
4. **What you want next**, connected to this role: "I'm looking for larger-scale distributed systems work, which is why this platform team appeals to me."

</Steps>

## Your project from six angles

Prepare a two-minute overview, then be ready for the interviewer to zoom into any of these:

| Angle | Be ready to explain | Typical follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| **Architecture** | components and why each exists | "Why Kafka rather than a REST call?" |
| **Request flow** | one important request, end to end, with rough numbers (QPS, latency) | "What happens if step 3 fails?" |
| **Data** | key tables/collections, one indexing or modelling decision | "How do you avoid duplicates? How big is that table?" |
| **Security** | authentication, authorisation, secrets, sensitive data | "How does a service know which tenant a request is for?" |
| **Deployment & operations** | CI/CD, environments, monitoring, an incident | "How do you know it's healthy? How do you roll back?" |
| **A hard problem** | the story below | "What would you do differently?" |

Draw it: a box-and-arrow diagram of five to eight components is easier to discuss than words. Know your numbers roughly — requests per second, data size, latency, team size.

## Telling the hard-problem story

<Steps>

1. **Situation** — context in two sentences, with the stakes: "0.1% of payments were charged twice."
2. **Why it was hard** — what made it non-obvious: intermittent, cross-service, no logs.
3. **What you did** — investigation steps and the options you considered, including the ones you rejected and why.
4. **Result** — measured: before and after, and how you verified it.
5. **Reflection** — what you learned or would change.

</Steps>

## Honesty and ownership

- Say **"I"** for what you did and **"we"** for the team. Interviewers probe exactly where your contribution ends; overclaiming collapses under two follow-ups.
- "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" is a strong answer. Inventing details is a fail.
- If the project was small, discuss it deeply anyway — and say how you'd scale it. Thoughtful reasoning about a modest system beats vague claims about a huge one.
- Confidentiality: describe architecture and numbers in rounded or relative terms; never share secrets or code.

## Common closing questions

| Question | A good answer |
|---|---|
| "Why are you looking to move?" | forward-looking and specific about what you want; never criticise your employer; if laid off, say so briefly and move on |
| "What would you change about the project?" | a real weakness and a concrete improvement — shows judgement |
| "Do you have questions for us?" | ask about the team's biggest technical challenge, on-call and incident handling, how decisions are made, what success looks like after six months |

<Pitfalls>

- **Listing every technology** in the introduction.
- **Describing the company's product instead of your work.**
- **No numbers** — "it was faster", "a lot of traffic".
- **Saying "we" for everything**, or "I" for everything.
- **Not knowing why a component exists** ("it was already there").
- **Badmouthing a previous employer or team.**
- **A rehearsed monologue** that doesn't adapt when the interviewer interrupts.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- A short, focused introduction steers the interview to your strengths.
- Prepare one project from six angles, with a diagram and rough numbers.
- Tell one hard problem with stakes, options, measured results and reflection.
- Be precise about your own contribution, and honest about gaps.

</KeyTakeaways>
