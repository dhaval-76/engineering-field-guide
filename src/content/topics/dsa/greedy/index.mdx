## Why it matters

A greedy algorithm builds a solution one step at a time, always taking the choice that looks best **right now**, and never reconsidering. When that works it is the simplest and fastest possible solution — usually a sort and a single pass, O(n log n) or O(n). Huffman compression, <Term id="dijkstra">Dijkstra</Term>'s shortest paths, Kruskal's and Prim's spanning trees, interval scheduling and many load-balancing heuristics are greedy.

The catch: greedy is **often wrong**, and a wrong greedy looks just as convincing as a right one. What separates a strong answer is the ability to *justify* the greedy choice — or to find the small counter-example that kills it and switch to [dynamic programming](../dynamic-programming/).

<Callout type="analogy">
Packing for a flight with a weight limit when every item is equally useful: take the lightest items first and you fit the most. That simple rule is provably best here — but if items had different values, "lightest first" could leave the most valuable thing behind, and you'd need to compare combinations (<Term id="dynamic-programming">dynamic programming</Term>).
</Callout>

## Recognise it

<Callout type="spot">

**Wording clues**

- A sort-then-scan feels natural: intervals by end, tasks by deadline, items by ratio, people by height.
- You can keep a single summary of the past ("farthest reachable index", "current fuel", "last occurrence of each letter").
- Choosing the extreme option (earliest end, largest, most frequent) leaves the most freedom for the rest.
- Local constraints (each element against its neighbours) that can be satisfied in one or two passes.

**Constraint clues**

- n up to 10⁵–10⁶ → O(n) or O(n log n) is expected, which rules out DP over pairs; greedy usually fits.
- "Return the minimum number of…" with a natural ordering of the input.

**Looks like it, but isn't**

- A small counterexample breaks the local choice (coins 1, 3, 4 for amount 6: greedy takes 4 + 1 + 1, the best is 3 + 3) → [dynamic programming](../dynamic-programming/).
- "Return **all** ways" → [backtracking](../backtracking/).

</Callout>

## How to justify a greedy choice

Two standard arguments, both short enough to say in an interview:

- **Exchange argument.** Take any optimal solution that does *not* make the greedy choice. Show you can swap the greedy choice in without making it worse. Then some optimal solution agrees with greedy at step one, and by repeating the argument, at every step. *Interval scheduling:* replace the first interval of any optimal schedule with the interval that ends earliest — it ends no later, so nothing after it conflicts.
- **Greedy stays ahead.** Show that after every step, greedy's partial solution is at least as good as any other algorithm's after the same number of steps (it has reached at least as far, used no more resources). *Jump game:* after k jumps, greedy's farthest reach is ≥ the reach of any k-jump strategy.

And one practical habit: **test the rule against an exact solver** on many small inputs before trusting it. The snippet below does exactly that for "largest coin first".

## Worked problems

### Jump game I and II

**Problem.** `nums[i]` is the maximum jump from index i. Can you reach the end (I)? With the fewest jumps (II)?

<Clues>only "**how far** can I reach?" matters → track the farthest reachable index; for fewest jumps, count BFS-like levels.</Clues>

**Insight.** For I, keep the **farthest index reachable so far**; if you ever stand on an index beyond it, you're stuck. For II, think in <Term id="bfs">BFS</Term> layers without a queue: all indices reachable with k jumps form a contiguous range; the next range ends at the farthest point reachable from it. Count how many times you must move to a new range.

### Gas station

**Problem.** Stations on a circle with `gas[i]` and `cost[i]` to reach the next. Find the start that completes the loop.

<Clues>**circular** route + "a start that completes the loop" → if the total gas ≥ total cost, start after the last point where the running tank went negative.</Clues>

**Insight.** If total gas < total cost, no start works. Otherwise, run once with a tank; whenever it goes negative at station i, **no station from the current start up to i can be a valid start** (each would arrive at i with even less fuel), so restart after i. The last restart point is the answer — one pass instead of trying every start.

### Partition labels

**Problem.** Split a string into as many parts as possible so each letter appears in only one part.

<Clues>"each letter in **one part** only" → record each letter's **last** index; close a part when you reach the furthest last index seen.</Clues>

**Insight.** Record each letter's **last** occurrence. Scan, extending the current part's end to the last occurrence of every letter seen; when the scan reaches that end, the part is closed. Cutting any earlier would split a letter; cutting later wastes a possible part.

### Task scheduler with cooldown

**Problem.** Identical tasks must be at least n slots apart. Minimum total slots?

<Clues>"minimum **slots** with a cooldown" → the most frequent task fixes the frame: `(maxCount − 1) × (n + 1) + tasksWithMaxCount`, or the number of tasks if that is larger.</Clues>

**Insight.** The most frequent task dictates the frame: `(maxCount − 1)` gaps of size `n + 1`, plus the tasks tied for the maximum in the final slot. If there are enough other tasks to fill every gap, no idling is needed and the answer is just the number of tasks: `max(tasks, (maxCount − 1)(n + 1) + tiedCount)`.

### Candy (two passes)

**Problem.** Children in a row with ratings each get at least one candy, and a child rated higher than a neighbour gets more than that neighbour. Minimum total? `[1,0,2]` → 5.

<Clues>each child compared only with **neighbours** → one left-to-right pass and one right-to-left pass, taking the maximum.</Clues>

**Insight.** Each child has two constraints, one per neighbour, and satisfying both at once is awkward. So satisfy them **one direction at a time**: a left-to-right pass gives each child one more than its left neighbour when rated higher; a right-to-left pass does the same for right neighbours, taking the max so the first pass isn't undone. Each pass only raises values as much as forced, so the total is minimal.

## When greedy fails

| Problem | Tempting greedy | Why it fails | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coin change (arbitrary coins) | largest coin first | {1, 3, 4}, amount 6 → 4+1+1 (3 coins) vs 3+3 (2) | DP |
| 0/1 knapsack | best value/weight first | items can't be split; ratios mislead | DP |
| Longest path | take the longest next edge | local choices block better routes | DP on DAGs / search |
| Interval scheduling | shortest interval first | a short interval can overlap two compatible long ones | earliest end ✓ |
| Fractional knapsack | best value/weight first | — works, items are divisible | greedy ✓ |
| Coin change, real currencies | largest coin first | — works: currencies are designed to be "canonical" | greedy ✓ |

## Talking through it in an interview

- State the greedy rule in one sentence, then justify it (exchange argument or stays-ahead).
- Try two or three small counter-examples out loud — interviewers often have one ready.
- If you find a counter-example, say so and switch to DP; that is a strong signal, not a failure.
- Mention the sort (O(n log n)) and the single pass.

<Pitfalls>

- **Assuming greedy works** without an argument or a counter-example search.
- **Sorting by the wrong key** (start instead of end for interval scheduling, shortest-first).
- **Satisfying two-sided constraints in one pass** — use two passes (candy, trapping rain water).
- **Ignoring ties** that affect correctness.
- **Overflow** in running sums.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Greedy commits to local choices; justify them with an exchange argument or "greedy stays ahead".
- Track compact state: farthest reach, running balance, last occurrence.
- Test small counter-examples against an exact solver; fall back to DP when choices interact.

</KeyTakeaways>
