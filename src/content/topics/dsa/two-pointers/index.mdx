## Why it matters

The brute force for "find two elements that…" is a pair of nested loops: O(n²) pairs. With a million elements that is 10¹² checks. Two pointers solve many of these problems in **one linear pass** by exploiting structure — usually sorted order or symmetry — to throw away a candidate at every step without checking it against everything else.

The same idea also rearranges arrays **in place**: one pointer reads, another writes, and no second array is needed. That matters when memory is tight and is exactly how `filter`-style operations work inside databases and standard libraries.

<Callout type="analogy">
Two people searching a sorted bookshelf for two books whose page counts add up to 1,000: one starts at the thinnest, one at the thickest. Too many pages? The person at the thick end moves inward. Too few? The thin end moves. Neither ever needs to go back — every step rules out a book for good.
</Callout>

## Recognise it

- **Sorted** array and a pair/triplet condition (sum, difference, closest) → converging pointers.
- **Symmetry** (palindromes, reversing, comparing both ends) → converging pointers.
- **Rearrange in place** with O(1) extra space (remove, deduplicate, move zeros, partition) → read/write pointers.
- **Two limits that shrink toward each other** (container with most water, trapping rain water) → converging pointers, moving the side that limits the answer.
- Linked-list cycles and middles → fast/slow pointers ([Linked lists](../linked-lists/)).
- A **contiguous** range with a validity condition → that's a [sliding window](../sliding-window/), the same-direction cousin of this pattern.

## Converging pointers: why it works

<Walkthrough id="converging-pointers" />

Take a sorted array and `sum = a[l] + a[r]` against a target:

- **Sum too small** → every pair `(l, j)` with `j < r` is even smaller (all those `a[j] ≤ a[r]`). So `l` can't be part of any answer: discard it, `l++`.
- **Sum too large** → by the same argument `r` can't be part of any answer: `r--`.
- **Equal** → found.

Each step permanently eliminates one index, and there are n of them: **O(n)**, with no extra memory. That elimination argument is what you should say out loud in an interview — it's what makes the greedy pointer moves correct, not just fast.

## Worked problems

### Valid palindrome

**Problem.** Is a string a palindrome, ignoring case and non-alphanumeric characters? `"A man, a plan, a canal: Panama"` → true.

**Insight.** Compare from both ends inward, skipping characters that don't count. No need to build a cleaned copy: O(1) extra space.

<CodeTabs id="dsa-valid-palindrome" title="Valid palindrome — converging pointers, O(1) space" />

**Follow-up:** "valid if you may delete at most one character" — on the first mismatch, try skipping either the left or the right character and check the rest normally: still O(n).

### 3Sum

**Problem.** All unique triplets that sum to zero. `[-1,0,1,2,-1,-4]` → `[[-1,-1,2],[-1,0,1]]`.

**Insight.** Sort. For each index `i`, the remaining problem is "pair in `nums[i+1..]` summing to `−nums[i]`" — converging pointers. Duplicates are the real difficulty: skip a value of `i` equal to the previous one, and after a match skip equal values at `l` and `r`.

| i (value) | l, r scan | triplets found |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (−4) | needs 4: no pair | — |
| 1 (−1) | needs 1: (−1, 2), (0, 1) | [−1,−1,2] [−1,0,1] |
| 2 (−1) | same value as i = 1 → skip | — |
| 3 (0) | needs 0: (1, 2) too big | — |

Sorted array: `[-4,-1,-1,0,1,2]`.

<CodeTabs id="dsa-three-sum" title="3Sum — sort + fix one + two pointers, O(n²)" />

**Complexity.** O(n²) — n choices of `i` × an O(n) scan — which is optimal for listing triplets in the worst case. **Generalising:** k-sum fixes k − 2 values with nested loops and finishes with two pointers: O(n^(k−1)).

### Container with most water

**Problem.** Given line heights, pick two lines that hold the most water: `area = (r − l) × min(h[l], h[r])`.

**Insight.** Start with the widest container. The **shorter** line limits the area. Moving the taller line inward makes the width smaller and can't raise the limit — that can never help. So always move the shorter line: it is the only move that might find a taller limit.

### Trapping rain water

**Problem.** Bars of given heights; how much rain is trapped between them? `[0,1,0,2,1,0,1,3,2,1,2,1]` → 6.

**Insight.** Water above bar `i` is `min(tallest to its left, tallest to its right) − height[i]`. Computing both maxima for every bar costs O(n) extra space (two prefix arrays). Two pointers avoid that: if `height[l] < height[r]`, there is a bar on the right at least as tall as `height[l]`, so the **left maximum alone** decides how much water sits on bar `l`. Settle bar `l` and move on; otherwise settle bar `r` from the right maximum.

<CodeTabs id="dsa-trapping-rain-water" title="Trapping rain water — settle the lower side, O(1) space" />

**Complexity.** O(n) time, O(1) space. (A monotonic stack also solves it in O(n), filling water layer by layer — see [Stacks](../stacks-and-monotonic-stacks/).)

### Sort colours (three-way partition)

**Problem.** Sort an array of 0s, 1s and 2s in one pass, in place.

**Insight.** Three pointers split the array into four regions: `[0, low)` are 0s, `[low, mid)` are 1s, `[mid, high]` is still unknown, `(high, end]` are 2s. Look at `nums[mid]`: a 0 swaps to the `low` region, a 1 stays, a 2 swaps to the `high` region — and `mid` does **not** advance after that last swap, because the value that came back from the end hasn't been examined yet.

This three-way partition is also how quicksort copes with many equal keys.

## Complexity at a glance

| Problem | Pointers | Time | Extra space |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pair with sum (sorted) | converging | O(n) | O(1) |
| Valid palindrome | converging | O(n) | O(1) |
| 3Sum | sort + converging | O(n²) | O(1)* |
| Container with most water | converging | O(n) | O(1) |
| Trapping rain water | converging | O(n) | O(1) |
| Remove duplicates | read/write | O(n) | O(1) |
| Sort colours | three-way | O(n) | O(1) |

\* plus the sort's own space, and the output.

## Talking through it in an interview

- Start with the brute force and its cost, then name the property you'll exploit ("the array is sorted, so…").
- Explain the **elimination argument** for converging pointers — why moving one pointer can't skip an answer.
- For in-place problems, state the invariant ("everything before `write` is kept").
- Ask whether the input may be modified or sorted, and whether original indices are needed (if so, sorting loses them — use a hash map instead).
- Watch the termination condition: `l < r` for pairs, `mid <= high` for partitions.

<Pitfalls>

- **Converging pointers on unsorted input** — the elimination argument needs order.
- **Duplicate results in k-sum** — skip equal values after sorting, at every level.
- **Off-by-one loop conditions** (`l < r` vs `l <= r`; `mid <= high` in the three-way partition).
- **Advancing `mid` after swapping with `high`** — the swapped-in value is skipped unchecked.
- **Moving the wrong side** in container problems — always move the side that limits the answer.
- **Sorting when original indices must be returned** — keep indices alongside values, or use hashing.

</Pitfalls>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Converging pointers eliminate one candidate per step on sorted or symmetric data.
- Read/write pointers rearrange in place with O(1) space; three pointers split into three groups.
- In "two walls" problems, move the side that limits the answer.
- Sort first to unlock the pattern; skip duplicates to avoid repeated answers.

</KeyTakeaways>
